# BM25 — Short Summary

## First, a citation note

"BM25 by Robertson and Sparck Jones" merges two different papers. Worth keeping straight:

| Paper | Authors | What it actually gave us |
|---|---|---|
| **Relevance weighting of search terms** (1976), *JASIS* 27, 129–146 | Robertson & Sparck Jones | The **RSJ weight** — the probabilistic term-weighting foundation, and the theoretical justification for IDF. **Not** BM25. |
| **Okapi at TREC-3** (1994), NIST SP 500-225, pp. 109–126 | Robertson, Walker, S. Jones, Hancock-Beaulieu, Gatford | **BM25 itself** — where the ranking function first appears in its familiar form. |
| **The Probabilistic Relevance Framework: BM25 and Beyond** (2009), *FnTIR* 3(4) | Robertson & Zaragoza | The retrospective that explains the derivation properly. Best single read if you want the reasoning. |

Sparck Jones is upstream of BM25 (via IDF and the 1976 weighting work), not a co-author of it.

---

## The core idea

BM25 ranks documents by estimating **P(relevant | document, query)** under the probabilistic relevance framework, assuming terms are independent. Each query term contributes a score, and the scores are summed.

The contribution of a term is built from three factors:

1. **How rare is the term?** → IDF-style component, descended directly from the 1976 RSJ weight.
2. **How often does it occur in this document?** → term frequency, but **saturating**.
3. **How long is the document?** → length normalization.

## The formula

$$\text{score}(D,Q) = \sum_{t \in Q} \text{IDF}(t) \cdot \frac{f(t,D)\,(k_1+1)}{f(t,D) + k_1\left(1 - b + b\,\frac{|D|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}$$

$$\text{IDF}(t) = \log\frac{N - n(t) + 0.5}{n(t) + 0.5}$$

Where `f(t,D)` is term frequency in the document, `|D|` is document length, `avgdl` is average document length across the collection, `N` is the number of documents, and `n(t)` is the number containing `t`.

## The two things that made it work

**Term frequency saturation (`k₁`).** Raw TF is wrong: the 10th occurrence of a word says much less than the 2nd. BM25 uses a rational function that rises steeply then flattens toward an asymptote of `k₁+1`. The theoretical motivation was the **2-Poisson / eliteness model** — the idea that a document is either "about" a term or not, and repeated occurrences quickly stop being evidence of which. Typical `k₁ ≈ 1.2–2.0`; smaller means faster saturation.

**Tunable length normalization (`b`).** Long documents contain more terms by accident, so they need penalizing — but not always fully. `b` interpolates: `b=0` disables normalization entirely, `b=1` normalizes fully by `|D|/avgdl`. The TREC-3 work landed on `b ≈ 0.75` as a good default, which the field has largely kept.

Earlier Okapi variants (BM1, BM11, BM15) were the fixed-end-point cases; BM25's contribution was essentially **parameterizing the interpolation between them** and finding the middle ground empirically outperformed both extremes.

A third parameter `k₃` handles query-term frequency, which only matters for long queries and is usually ignored.

## Why it stuck

- Empirically strong on TREC ad hoc retrieval, and it stayed strong for two decades.
- **No training required** — the collection statistics are all it needs, unlike learned rankers.
- Cheap to compute over postings lists, so it drops straight into an inverted-index engine. This is why it's the default similarity in Lucene/Elasticsearch (which replaced classic TF-IDF with BM25 in ES 5.x).

## Known limitations

- Pure lexical matching — no synonymy, no semantics. "car" and "automobile" are unrelated terms.
- Term independence assumption is plainly false.
- `k₁` and `b` are corpus-dependent; defaults are good, not optimal.
- Length normalization can over-penalize genuinely long relevant documents (addressed later by BM25+ / lower-bounding work).
- Single-field. **BM25F** (Robertson, Zaragoza & Taylor, 2004) extends it to weighted fields — title vs body vs anchor text — by combining term frequencies *before* saturation rather than scoring fields separately.

---

**Bottom line:** BM25 is TF-IDF with the two obvious flaws fixed — unbounded term frequency and naive length handling — derived from a probabilistic model rather than patched by intuition. It remains the baseline every new retrieval method is measured against, and the sparse half of most modern hybrid search stacks.
